Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about accessible housing, funding, and using this toolkit.

What is accessible housing? ▼

Accessible housing is designed to accommodate people with disabilities, allowing them to live independently and safely. This includes features like step-free entrances, wider doorways, accessible bathrooms, and barrier-free pathways. For more details, see the Accessible Housing section.

How do I find funding for home modifications? ▼

Funding is available through federal programs (like HATC tax credits), provincial programs, insurance (auto or workplace), and not-for-profit organizations. Start by selecting your province or territory from the dropdown menu at the top of the page, then visit the Financial Resources in Your Region section under Finances to see programs available to you.

Do I need to pay for modifications upfront? ▼

Many programs require you to pay upfront and submit receipts for reimbursement. Some programs require pre-approval before starting work. Always check specific program requirements before beginning any modifications. See the Important Reminders in the Financial Resources section for more details.

What are my rights as a tenant with a disability? ▼

You have the right to request reasonable modifications to your rental unit under human rights legislation. Landlords cannot refuse accommodations that are necessary for your accessibility needs, unless it causes undue hardship. Visit the Legal Rights section for detailed information about tenant rights and protections.

How do I select my region to see local resources? ▼

Use the Region dropdown menu at the top of the page (in the navigation bar) to select your province or territory. This will automatically update content throughout the toolkit to show region-specific programs, resources, and contact information.

Can I get help navigating funding programs? ▼

Yes! Free navigation support is available through services like 2-1-1 Canada, the Government of Canada Benefits Finder, Barrier-Free Benefits, and the Plan Institute Helpline. You can also connect with your regional SCI association for specialized peer support. See the Free Navigation Support section under Finances for contact information.

What is the difference between accessible, adaptable, and barrier-free housing? ▼

Accessible housing is designed for immediate use by people with disabilities. Adaptable housing can be easily modified in the future to meet changing needs. Barrier-free housing removes physical obstacles and allows full accessibility. Visit the Terminology section under Accessible Housing for complete definitions.

Who created this toolkit? ▼

This toolkit was created through a comprehensive research study exploring the housing needs and experiences of people with spinal cord injury and their families. The study involved interviews, surveys, and co-design workshops with people with lived experience, healthcare professionals, and community organizations. Learn more in the Research section.

How can I get peer support from someone with lived experience? ▼

Connect with your regional SCI association for peer support programs. Select your province or territory from the dropdown menu, then visit the Mental Health & Peer Support section to find your local SCI organization's contact information. Many organizations offer peer mentorship from people who have "been there, done that."

Background & Purpose

Access to appropriate and affordable housing is a human right and is critical for promoting health, dignity, safety, inclusion, and community participation.

Inaccessible housing can promote feelings of social isolation, lack of belonging, and make people feel like they have no control over their environment. No one deserves to feel this way in their home.

The housing needs of people with spinal cord injury have received little attention, and the needs of other family household members even less so. We recognize the importance of working together with and listening to people with spinal cord injury & their families in order to learn how to find and create homes that work.

Terminology

▼

Understanding Terminology, Codes, and Standards in Accessible Housing and Design

▼

Terms such as accessible housing, universal design, barrier-free, and adaptable are widely used in accessible housing, policy, and practice. Although these terms may sound clear, their meaning often depends on their context and how they are defined.

Finding the right home can be harder for people with disabilities because housing systems often use technical language. Understanding these terms can help people, families, and service providers ask better questions, compare options, and advocate for housing that truly meets their needs.

Two types of definitions help clarify what is intended: conceptual definitions and operational definitions.

Conceptual Definitions: The "Why"

A conceptual definition explains the purpose behind a term. It answers the question: What is the goal?

In housing and design, this may include:

  • Supporting independence
  • Promoting safety and dignity
  • Reducing barriers to participation
  • Accommodating a wide range of abilities
  • Allowing homes to respond to changing needs over time

Conceptual definitions focus on the values, principles, and intended outcomes of inclusive design. They describe what good housing should achieve for the people who live there.

Operational Definitions: The "How"

An operational definition explains how something is applied or measured in practice. It answers the question: What does this require in real-world terms?

In housing and design, this often includes measurable features such as minimum doorway widths, step-free entrances, defined turning spaces, reinforced bathroom walls for future grab bars, or specific reach ranges for controls. These are the technical requirements found in standards, guidelines, and building codes.

Operational definitions translate broad goals into concrete, observable elements that can be checked and measured.

How Requirements Are Written into Codes and Standards

Accessibility and design requirements are generally written in one of two ways: prescriptive or objective-based (performance-based).

Prescriptive requirements provide specific instructions about what must be built. They may state exact dimensions, slopes, or mounting heights. This approach focuses on following clearly defined and measurable rules.

Objective-based (or performance-based) requirements focus on the outcome that must be achieved. Instead of listing only exact measurements, they describe the intended result — such as providing safe and independent access or enabling use by people with a range of abilities. Designers and builders may have flexibility in how they meet these objectives, as long as they can demonstrate that the final result achieves the required level of performance.

Why This Matters

A home or building can meet technical requirements and still not fully support the people who use it. A doorway may meet the minimum width but be difficult to navigate because of layout constraints. A bathroom may technically include accessible features but not function well for the individual living there.

Meeting a standard does not automatically mean meeting real-life needs.

Understanding both the purpose behind inclusive housing and design (conceptual definitions) and the measurable requirements used to implement them (operational definitions) supports clearer communication, stronger advocacy, and better decision-making in housing, design, and policy.

Home Types

▼
  • Social Housing (Non-Market Housing)
    Housing funded or supported by governments and/or non-profit organizations. It is intended to be more affordable and may include public housing, co-operative housing, supportive group homes, and some long-term care or assisted living homes.
  • Private Market Housing
    Housing that is rented or owned through the open market, including private rentals, condominiums, and owned homes. Accessibility features may or may not be included unless required by law or added by the owner.

Supportive Housing

▼
What is Supportive Housing?
▼

Supportive housing combines affordable housing with on-site or coordinated support services designed to help people live independently in the community. Support services may include personal care, meal preparation, medication management, life skills training, and connection to community resources. Supportive housing differs from independent housing in that it provides structured support, but differs from institutional care (like nursing homes) in that residents maintain greater independence and control over their daily lives.

Examples of Supportive Housing
▼
  • Assisted Living / Retirement Homes
    Provide private or semi-private living spaces with shared meals, housekeeping, and some personal care. Support levels vary, and residents usually pay privately.
  • Long-Term Care (Nursing Homes)
    Provide 24-hour nursing and personal care for people with complex health needs. Care is regulated by the health system, and private space is usually limited.
  • Supportive Independent Living
    Allows a person to live in their own home while receiving services such as personal care, home care, or therapy from outside providers.
  • Group Homes (also called "Small Option" Homes)
    Shared homes where several people with disabilities live together and receive daily on-site support from staff.
  • Supportive Living Buildings
    Apartment or condominium buildings with on-site care or support services, allowing people to live independently while accessing supports as needed.
  • Transitional Housing
    Temporary housing with supports for people transitioning from hospitals, shelters, or unstable housing situations while preparing for longer-term housing.

Design Guides & Standards

Canadian National Accessibility Standards

▼

Canada has developed national accessibility standards that apply across the country. These standards provide technical requirements and design specifications for creating accessible housing.

  • CSA/ASC B652 - Accessible Design for the Built Environment
    Canadian standard providing technical requirements for accessible design in buildings and exterior spaces.
  • CSA B651 - Accessible Design for Buildings
    Standard for accessible building design, including entrances, interior spaces, and facilities.
  • CAN-ASC-2.8 - Technical Requirements for Housing
    Canadian accessibility standard for housing design and construction.
  • CAN-ASC-2.2 - Emergency Egress (Exit) Standard
    Provides requirements for emergency exits and evacuation procedures that accommodate people with disabilities.

Certification & Training Programs

▼

Building Certification Programs

▼

Building certification programs evaluate and rate the accessibility of buildings, homes, and developments. These programs help identify truly accessible properties and encourage developers to exceed minimum code requirements.

  • Rick Hansen Foundation Accessibility Certification (RHFAC)
    Canadian program that measures and certifies the accessibility of buildings and sites. The RHF Accessibility Certification™ (RHFAC) Rating evaluates buildings on a scale from 0 to 100 based on accessibility features.
  • SAFERHome
    Canadian certification program specifically for residential housing, focusing on safety, accessibility, functional design, and environmental sustainability.

Professional Training Programs

▼

Professional training programs help builders, contractors, occupational therapists, and real estate professionals understand accessible housing design, modification, and best practices.

  • AdaptivHOME Course (CHBA)
    Canadian Home Builders' Association training for building and home modification professionals on accessible and adaptable housing design.
  • Rick Hansen Foundation Accessibility Training
    Canadian training programs covering accessibility awareness, design, and certification. Various offerings for different professional roles.
  • Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist (CAPS)
    American certification for building and home modification professionals specializing in aging-in-place renovations and accessible design (relevant for Canadian professionals working near the border or on cross-border projects).
  • Seniors Real Estate Specialist (SRES)
    Real Estate Institute of Canada designation for realtors specializing in helping seniors and people with accessibility needs find suitable housing.

Planning for Changing Needs

▼

Your housing needs may change over time as your life circumstances evolve. Planning ahead can help you make informed decisions about modifications, relocations, and long-term housing strategies.

  • Anticipating Future Needs
    Consider how your mobility, care needs, and lifestyle may change over time. Think about features that could accommodate future changes, such as wider doorways, adaptable bathroom spaces, and accessible entrances.
  • Flexible Design Features
    Universal design and adaptable features allow your home to grow with you. Examples include reinforced bathroom walls for future grab bar installation, adjustable countertops, and flexible room layouts.
  • Long-Term Planning Tools
    Work with occupational therapists and accessibility advisors to create a long-term housing plan. Consider creating a modification timeline and budget that accounts for both immediate and future needs.
  • Family & Support Considerations
    Think about how your care and support needs may evolve. Consider proximity to family, caregiver spaces, and the availability of home care services in your area.

Neighbourhood & Location

▼

The neighbourhood you choose is just as important as the home itself. Consider accessibility features in your community, proximity to services, transportation options, and the overall environment.

  • Community Accessibility
    Look for neighbourhoods with accessible sidewalks, curb cuts, pedestrian crossings, and well-maintained pathways. Consider the availability of accessible parks, recreation facilities, and public spaces.
  • Proximity to Services
    Evaluate distance to essential services like healthcare facilities, pharmacies, grocery stores, and rehabilitation centers. Consider both current and future accessibility of these services.
  • Transportation Options
    Assess available transportation, including accessible public transit routes, HandyDART or paratransit services, and the ease of accessible taxi or ride-sharing options. Consider parking availability and proximity for both yourself and visitors.
  • Social & Recreational Opportunities
    Look for accessible community centers, libraries, cultural venues, and social programs. Consider peer support groups, recreation programs, and opportunities for community engagement.
  • Climate & Weather Considerations
    Think about how local climate affects accessibility. Consider winter snow clearing practices, drainage during rain, and heat management in summer. Some neighbourhoods have better maintenance and snow removal services than others.
  • Building a Support Network
    Consider the presence of disability-friendly communities, neighbours, and local support networks. Research local advocacy groups, peer support organizations, and community resources.

Location Checklist

When evaluating a neighbourhood, consider visiting at different times of day and in different weather conditions to get a complete picture of accessibility and livability.

Accessibility in Strata Developments ▼

Accessibility in Strata Developments

Resource Purpose

This resource supports strata councils, property managers, owners, and advisors with lawful, consistent, and timely responses to disability-related accessibility requests.

Practical Design & Policy Tips

  • Build accessibility flexibility into bylaws and renovation policies
  • Keep a standard accommodation request form
  • Train council members on human rights and legal obligations
  • Remember: the goal is barrier removal, not strict rule enforcement

Checklist for Accessibility Requests

1) Intake & Acknowledgement

  • Acknowledge the request promptly and respectfully
  • Confirm the request relates to a disability-related need (only as necessary)
  • Assign a point person and timeline for response

2) Information Gathering (Proportionate)

  • Clarify the barrier created by the existing rule, design, or practice
  • Understand the functional need (not the diagnosis)
  • Request supporting information only if needed (e.g., brief professional note)

3) Accommodation Options (Explore Before Deciding)

  • Identify multiple reasonable options (temporary, partial, or permanent)
  • Consider location, design, safety, and maintenance implications
  • Consider cost-sharing where appropriate (do not assume the owner must pay)

4) Undue Hardship Analysis (Evidence-Based)

  • Is the rule or policy legitimate and necessary?
  • Was it adopted in good faith?
  • Can the objective be met while accommodating?
  • Assess actual hardship (significant cost, safety risk, or structural infeasibility)
  • Note: inconvenience, aesthetics, or precedent are not undue hardship

5) Decision & Documentation

  • Approve accommodation to the point of undue hardship
  • If modifying the request, explain why and propose alternatives
  • Document rationale, evidence considered, and conditions (if any)

6) Implementation & Review

  • Coordinate permits, contractors, and access
  • Set clear responsibilities for maintenance and repair
  • Schedule review if needs may change over time

Best Practice Examples

Key Shift: Accessibility requests are human rights accommodation obligations â€“ not favours or bylaw exceptions.
  • Instead of: Enforcing bylaws first
    Do: Identify the barrier created by the rule or design
  • Instead of: Asking for excessive medical details
    Do: Request only what's needed to understand the functional limitation
  • Instead of: Assuming the owner pays all costs
    Do: Consider shared responsibility for common property
  • Instead of: Denying requests due to aesthetics or precedent
    Do: Focus on evidence-based undue hardship
  • Instead of: Treating accessibility as static
    Do: Plan for changing needs over time

Understanding "Undue Hardship"

Undue hardship is the legal limit on a strata corporation's duty to accommodate under human rights law. The burden of proving undue hardship rests with the strata corporation â€“ not the person requesting accommodation.

What Does Not Count as Undue Hardship

  • Inconvenience to other residents
  • Concerns about setting a precedent
  • Minor or moderate costs
  • Aesthetic impacts
  • Administrative effort
  • Generalized safety concerns without evidence

What May Count as Undue Hardship

  • Serious safety risks that cannot be mitigated
  • Structural infeasibility confirmed by professionals
  • Excessive costs threatening financial viability
  • Legal or regulatory impossibility

Key Takeaway

The question is not whether accommodation is inconvenient or costly â€“ it is whether denying it is legally defensible. A cooperative, evidence-based approach that actively seeks solutions is the strongest protection for strata corporations.

FAQ: Working with Service Providers

When should I contact an Occupational Therapist? ▼

An occupational therapist can help at several stages:

  • Before purchasing or renting to assess accessibility
  • When planning home modifications
  • To provide recommendations for funding applications
  • To work with contractors on specific needs

Many funding programs require an occupational therapist assessment, so connect early in the process.

How long does it take to find a contractor and complete renovations? ▼

Finding a contractor: Can take weeks to months, especially in rural areas or for accessibility-specialized work.

Timeline for work: Even after securing a contractor, projects often take 6+ months to complete.

Plan ahead: If you're in hospital planning discharge, understand that modifications may not be complete before you return home. Consider temporary solutions or transitional housing.

Newly Injured with a Spinal Cord Injury: Planning for an Accessible Home

If you or someone you love has recently experienced a spinal cord injury (SCI), you are likely navigating an overwhelming amount of information, decisions, and emotions. Whether your injury is recent or you are preparing to leave rehabilitation, thinking about home accessibility can feel both urgent and daunting.

This page will help you think through temporary versus permanent home modifications, realistic timelines, the importance of timing major renovations, and the emotional side of adjusting to life after SCI.

Transitional Housing: Living Somewhere Temporarily While You Wait

▼

After a spinal cord injury, you may not be able to return immediately to your home — or to any permanent home. Accessible housing is in short supply in most communities, and making a home accessible takes time. Equipment needs may still be evolving. Funding applications can take months. Sometimes the right long-term home simply isn't ready yet.

In these situations, transitional housing — a temporary place to live while longer-term arrangements are being made — may be necessary. This is common and does not mean you have failed to plan.

Where People Sometimes Stay Temporarily

  • Extended-stay hotels or accessible hotel rooms — Some hotels have roll-in showers and accessible features. Costs can add up quickly, but this may be a short-term bridge.
  • Retirement or assisted living homes — Some people temporarily move into a retirement or assisted living home while waiting for a permanent accessible home to be modified or located. While these settings are not designed for younger people with SCI, they can provide care and safety during a transition period.
  • Rehabilitation facilities or transitional care units — Some hospitals or rehab centres have transitional care beds for people who are medically stable but cannot yet go home safely.
  • Staying with family or friends — This is common but requires careful thought about space, caregiver strain, privacy, and accessibility of the temporary environment.
  • Adapted short-term rentals — Some accessible short-term rentals are available through platforms like Airbnb Accessibility or WheelchairTraveling.com.

⚠️ Things to Consider for Any Temporary Arrangement

  • Is the space physically accessible for your current mobility needs?
  • Can you get in and out safely — including in an emergency?
  • Is personal care support available, or will family/friends provide it?
  • Is there a realistic timeline and plan for the next step?
  • What is the cost, and is there any funding available to help?

Transitional situations can be emotionally difficult — especially when they feel prolonged or uncertain. Connecting with your regional SCI association or a social worker can help you navigate options and advocate for solutions.

1. Temporary vs. Permanent Modifications: Understanding the Trade-Offs

▼

In the first 6 to 18 months after a spinal cord injury, your body is still healing and adapting. Swelling decreases, nerves stabilize, and patterns such as spasticity become clearer. At the same time, rehabilitation can lead to major functional gains. You may build strength and endurance, improve balance, master new transfer techniques, or transition between mobility devices.

Because of this period of change, many clinicians recommend beginning with flexible or temporary solutions before committing to major structural renovations.

Temporary Modifications

Temporary solutions might include portable ramps, shower chairs or tub transfer benches, over-the-toilet commodes, removable grab bars, bed rails, furniture rearrangement, or small threshold ramps.

These options are often:

  • Less expensive upfront
  • Faster to install
  • Easier to remove or adjust
  • Useful while waiting for funding or contractor availability

However, temporary modifications can come with challenges. They may feel less stable or less integrated into the home. Some require more energy to use or still involve caregiver assistance. Equipment can take up significant space, especially in bathrooms and bedrooms. In shared households, temporary ramps, commodes, and mobility equipment may obstruct walkways, reduce privacy, or disrupt routines for partners, children, or roommates. This can create tension or frustration—not because anyone is unsupportive, but because space and daily flow are affected.

Temporary solutions are often a bridge. They allow safety and function while your long-term needs become clearer.

Permanent Modifications

Permanent changes may include widening doorways, installing a roll-in shower, lowering counters, adding ceiling lifts, building exterior ramps, or reconfiguring rooms.

These renovations can significantly improve independence, safety, and energy conservation. They may reduce caregiver strain and make daily routines more efficient. But they also require higher upfront investment, longer planning timelines, and construction disruption. Once completed, they are difficult or costly to reverse.

If major renovations are completed too early, they may reflect your abilities during early rehabilitation rather than your long-term capacity.

For example, someone who initially requires a mechanical lift may later become independent with transfers. Bathroom layouts designed for full caregiver assistance may not support independence later. Counter heights chosen for one mobility device may not fit another if equipment changes.

Timing matters.

2. Why Waiting Can Sometimes Be the Right Decision

▼

It can feel urgent to "fix everything" immediately after discharge. Living in an inaccessible space is exhausting and sometimes unsafe. At the same time, the early recovery period is often marked by meaningful functional change.

Depending on your injury level and severity, you may:

  • Improve transfer independence
  • Transition between mobility devices
  • Develop more efficient self-care routines
  • Build endurance and confidence

Waiting before committing to major structural changes allows your rehabilitation progress to inform smarter, longer-term decisions.

Waiting is not inaction. It can involve using temporary solutions, tracking your progress, reassessing with an occupational therapist, and planning renovations in phases rather than all at once.

3. Realistic Timelines: Modifying or Finding an Accessible Home

▼

Accessibility changes often take longer than people expect.

Modifying an existing home typically involves assessment, funding applications, contractor scheduling, permits, and construction. Even when everyone is motivated, the full process often takes several months and sometimes a year or more.

Searching for a new accessible home can take even longer. Truly accessible properties are limited in many communities. Rentals may be competitive, and purchased homes often still require modifications after move-in. It is not uncommon for the full process—from searching to final renovation—to take 6 to 18 months.

Understanding this timeline can help reduce the shock and frustration when things do not move quickly. Many people move through stages: immediate safety fixes, short-term temporary adaptations, and then more permanent renovations once needs and funding are clearer.

4. Adjustment, Identity, and Hope for the Future

▼

Home represents privacy, control, and identity. After a spinal cord injury, it can be painful to realize that your own space no longer works for your body. It is common to feel grief, frustration, anger, fear, or exhaustion. You may also feel pressure to make the "right" long-term decisions quickly.

It is okay if this feels overwhelming.

You are adjusting physically, emotionally, and practically—all at once. Some days will feel hopeful. Others may feel heavy. Adjustment is rarely linear.

At the same time, a spinal cord injury does not eliminate the possibility of a meaningful and fulfilling life. People with SCI build careers, families, relationships, creative pursuits, and community involvement. Your independence six months from now may look different than it does today. Your independence years from now may look different again.

An accessible home is one foundation for the life you are continuing to build. And you do not have to solve everything immediately.

It is reasonable to:

  • Start with temporary solutions
  • Reassess as your skills improve
  • Make changes in phases
  • Consider the needs of others in your household while also advocating for your own safety and independence

Your injury is part of your story—but it is not the whole story.

With time, thoughtful planning, and support, your home can evolve alongside you—creating a space that supports your growth, your relationships, and a future that still holds connection, purpose, and possibility.
